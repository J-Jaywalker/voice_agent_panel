# PART ONE: THE JOURNEY TO 2030

**TARGET TIME 5 MINS**

**RICARDO**

Morning.

I’m Ricardo Herreros Symons, CRO at Speechmatics.

But for the next twenty minutes, I’ll be your personal Dr Emmet Brown, and will take you back to the future.

*[HOLDS UP PHONE.]*

If we’re going to do some time travelling, this is probably the closest thing I have to a DeLorean. No gull-wing doors. No flux capacitor. But it does fit in my pocket.

*[BEAT.]*

And actually, this little thing contains almost the whole story of how communication has changed. Calls. Messages. Apps. Assistants. Now agents. So if we want to understand where all of this is going next, we need to rewind first.

Let’s go back.

*[PAUSE.]*

At first, a phone connected one human to another. Then businesses scaled the human with call centres. Then we started automating the conversation. Press one for sales. Press two for support. Press three to hear those options again because apparently once was not enough.

*[BEAT FOR LAUGH.]*

Then chatbots replaced menus with language. For a while, their greatest achievement was helping you find the button marked: “Speak to a human.” Then the models learned to hold context. Reason. Use tools. And suddenly software wasn’t just answering us. It was acting for us. That’s where we are today. 2026.

A chatbot can tell you when your insurance policy expires. An agent can renew it. A chatbot can show you flights. An agent can book one. A chatbot can tell you to contact your bank. An agent can increasingly do some of that work for you.

Software is moving from answering to acting. Which changes the question. We used to ask: Can it understand me? Then: Can it answer me? Now: What can it do for me? And immediately after that:  What is it allowed to do?

*[PAUSE.]*

We’ve seen how we got here. Now let’s go somewhere we haven’t been. 2030.

Because the next step is not simply a better agent. It is agents dealing with other agents. Your agent communicating with your bank’s agent. Your company’s agent negotiating with a supplier’s agent. A healthcare agent coordinating across systems before you ever arrive at the clinic. And when machines communicate with machines, they don’t need to sit there politely synthesising vowels at each other. They can communicate directly.

What matters is that they can discover one another, exchange information, negotiate and act. Machines communicating without humans is not new. Trading systems have done it. Industrial systems have done it. Plenty of less cheerful systems have done it too. What changes here is that these systems can increasingly reason, negotiate and act on our behalf while they do it.

So the journey becomes: Human to human. Human to machine. Human to agent. And now: Agent to agent. Which raises a much more interesting question. What actually happens when the person on the other end of the line isn’t a person anymore... and neither are you?

I could speculate. But we’ve come all the way to 2030. So let’s ask them.

# PART FOUR: BACK TO 2026

**SCREEN**

*Panel muted.*

**RICARDO**

Right. Back to 2026. So what did we actually see? I think there are three things worth taking home.

First: Agents will increasingly deal with other agents. That changes the shape of customer service, operations and enterprise software. It doesn’t mean humans vanish. It means we don’t necessarily have to sit inside every administrative exchange ourselves. Our software can increasingly carry our intent into other systems.

Second: Understanding becomes part of the control system. In a world of agents, speech recognition cannot stop at transcription. It has to help answer: Who spoke? Did they finish? Was that a correction? Was that an interruption? Did they change language? Was that instruction stable enough to act on? Because once speech can trigger an action... getting the words right is not simply a transcription problem anymore. It is part of the safety system.

*[PAUSE.]*

And third: The human moves. Today, we spend an extraordinary amount of time operating technology. Opening apps. Filling in forms. Repeating information. Calling companies. Navigating menus.

Following up. In a more agentic world, we increasingly describe the outcome instead. “Renew this, but don’t spend more than £1,500.” “Find me an appointment, but not on Tuesday.” “Resolve this claim, but bring me in if there’s a dispute.”

The machine handles more of the mechanics. The human sets the intent. The limits. The judgement. The accountability. We move from operating every workflow... towards governing more of them. And I think that distinction matters. Because when people hear “agent-to-agent”, it is easy to jump immediately to: Where are the humans?

The answer is not necessarily that they disappear. It is that they stop having to occupy every step.

*[PAUSE.]*

Of course, the moment we give software more freedom to act, we also create a much bigger security problem. The attack surface is no longer just the prompt. It is identity. Permissions. Memory. Audio. The other agent. The tool. The action that comes next. Which means the next few years are not simply a race to build smarter models. The models will get smarter. The difficult work is everything around them. Making identity reliable. Making permissions granular. Making systems interoperable without making them gullible. Making decisions auditable. Knowing when to act. Knowing when not to act. And knowing when a human needs to come back in.

*[PAUSE.]*

That is also where ASR sits in this future. The understanding underneath it. Because before intelligence can reason about what you said... it has to know what you actually said. Before an agent can act for you... it has to understand you. And the more autonomy we give these systems... the more expensive a misunderstood sentence can become.

*[PAUSE.]*

Twenty minutes ago, this was our DeLorean.

*[HOLDS UP PHONE.]*

We used it to go backwards and see how we got here. Then four years forward. And the strange thing is... we didn’t see anything that requires magic. We saw technologies that already exist becoming good enough to trust together.

Agents that reason. Agents that use tools. Agents that take actions. Systems that can interact with other systems. Voice that is becoming more fluid, interruptible and natural. Security systems trying to keep up with all of it.

2030 is not waiting for one magical invention. It is waiting for these pieces to become reliable enough to trust each other. Which is why 2030 doesn’t feel particularly futuristic to me anymore. It feels like an engineering deadline.

*[PAUSE.]*

And maybe that is what The Last Human in the Loop actually means. Not the last person desperately hanging onto a workflow while the machines take over. The person who decides what the system is allowed to do. The person who sets the boundary.

The person who carries the judgement. And the person the system knows when to bring back in. So the question isn’t whether humans will remain inside every loop.

They won’t.

The more useful question is:

Can we build the loops now so that we know when a human still needs to be there?

Thank you.
